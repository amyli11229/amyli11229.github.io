## Background

Summer 2025, I joined UBC's Emerging Media Lab as the UX/UI Designer. The lab focuses on experimental technologies such as VR, AI, and immersive media.

One of the first projects I worked on was Procedural Poetry Funhouse, an immersive VR experience inspired by the visual poetry of Judith Copithorne. Interestingly, I later discovered that Judith shares the exact same birthday as my grandmother, which made the project feel unexpectedly personal and gave me an even stronger appreciation for her work.

The project was selected for approximately $100,000 in funding for the next two years, making it one of the lab's flagship research projects. Because of its long-term vision, every design decision needed to establish a solid foundation for future development.

As the UX/UI designer, I was responsible for defining the overall user experience, interaction design, interface system, and collaborating closely with artists and developers throughout the project.

## Challenge 1 — Rebuilding the Project

### Situation

When I joined the team, we made a major decision: instead of continuing with the previous prototype, we would migrate the entire project to a new development platform.

This meant many existing assets and interactions could not simply be transferred—we were essentially starting from scratch.

### Task

My responsibility was to redesign the user experience so that the new version wasn't simply a recreation of the old one, but an opportunity to build a more scalable and maintainable interaction system.

### Action

I first studied the previous prototype to understand which interactions worked well and which confused users.

Then I worked with developers and artists to redesign the overall interaction flow, organize information architecture, and build reusable UI components that could support future features.

Rather than designing isolated screens, I focused on creating a consistent interaction language that the team could continue expanding over the next two years.

### Result

This gave the team a much cleaner design foundation and significantly improved collaboration between design and engineering during development.

## Challenge 2 — Motion Sickness in VR

### Situation

One of the biggest usability challenges in VR is motion sickness.

During early testing, we noticed that users often felt uncomfortable when moving through the surreal poetic environment.

### Task

My goal was to preserve the magical feeling of exploration while minimizing discomfort.

### Action

I researched VR comfort guidelines and analyzed how successful VR experiences handled locomotion.

I experimented with different navigation methods, adjusted movement speed and acceleration, introduced visual reference points, and simplified interactions so users could focus on the poetry rather than learning complicated controls.

I continuously observed user testing sessions and iterated based on feedback.

### Result

The experience became noticeably more comfortable while maintaining the artistic atmosphere the team wanted to create.

## Overall Results

Over the course of approximately 12 months, I helped establish the UX direction for one of the lab's largest immersive research projects.

Beyond designing interfaces, I learned how UX design functions within interdisciplinary research, balancing artistic vision, technical constraints, and user needs simultaneously.

## Takeaway

This project fundamentally changed how I think about UX.

Before joining the lab, I viewed UX primarily as designing intuitive interfaces. Through this project, I realized that UX is really about translating abstract creative ideas into experiences that people can naturally understand and enjoy.

Working alongside artists, researchers, and engineers taught me how to communicate across disciplines, make design decisions under uncertainty, and iterate through continuous testing. Those lessons continue to shape how I approach every design project today.
